# First-principles research writing

Writing organizes a reader's reasoning. Begin with the question, the established result, and the evidence needed to connect them. The logical spine should remain visible when a reader skims opening sentences.

## Plan from evidence

Read the proof sources and corrected claims before outlining. Identify the intended audience's prior knowledge and the new understanding the paper delivers. Draft a short logical spine: problem → gap in prior results → precise contribution → mechanism/proof idea → consequences. Adapt the number of paragraphs and sections to those dependencies, not a decorative template.

Lock contributions, notation, theorem labels and sources before parallel writing. A section has a concrete job in the argument; a paragraph advances one step toward that job. Record the outline internally during autonomous execution rather than interrupting a previously authorized full run for routine outline approval.

## Draft logical paragraphs

Open with the paragraph's claim. Give the definitions, evidence, derivation or comparison needed to justify it. Close with a meaningful implication when useful; avoid recap or a vague pointer to the next section. Connect adjacent paragraphs through a shared object, result or unresolved question. If the chain has a missing inferential step, add that reasoning rather than a filler transition.

Explain concepts before attaching names or acronyms. Give one concept one consistent name. Prefer established terms over labels invented solely to make familiar mathematics sound new. Ground each factual or mathematical claim in earlier reasoning, appropriate common knowledge or a correct citation.

## Match precision to evidence

Scope every result to its actual domain, assumptions and quantifiers. Keep constants and boundary cases faithful to the proof. Do not promote an example to a universal claim, a sufficient condition to an iff, a computational check to a proof, or transcription fidelity to mathematical correctness.

Explain the mechanism with specific objects and steps: what is constructed, what invariant is maintained, what inequality is used, and why it applies. For proof passages, number substantive steps and give justifications. For narrative passages, use equations only when they clarify the central comparison; put full statements and assumptions in the formal sections.

## Revise for information gain

For every sentence, identify the new information or necessary reasoning it contributes. Cut repetition, empty importance markers, unnecessary self-description and undefined rhetoric. Prefer concrete verbs and short key claims. Preserve technical evidence, required limitations and honest verification status.

Auditors return exact locators, severity and concrete repairs; the coordinating writer edits. Read the revised section as a reader who has not seen the project history. Verify its logical chain, terminology, references and mathematical scope before independent final review.
